Autonomous Line-Following & BLE-Controlled Robot

Designed, fabricated, wired, and programmed a differential-drive mobile robot capable of autonomous black-line following and Bluetooth Low Energy wireless control as part of a two-person instrumentation and design course project.

01 · Project Overview

Building an Integrated Electromechanical System

This project was completed by a two-person team as part of an instrumentation and design course focused on integrating sensing, electronics, programming, and physical system design.

The objective was to build a mobile robot that could operate in two distinct modes: autonomous line following using onboard sensors, and wireless manual control through Bluetooth Low Energy.

We designed and assembled the complete platform ourselves, including the 3D-printed chassis and wheels, motors, batteries, sensors, mechanical fasteners, soldered electronics, and embedded control system.

The main challenge was integration. Mechanical layout affected sensor placement and wiring, sensor behavior affected control logic, and motor response affected whether the robot could reliably follow the intended path.

02 · System Architecture

Mechanical, Electrical & Software Integration

Mechanical Platform

Designed and assembled a compact 3D-printed chassis and wheel system around the motors, electronics, sensors, batteries, and mounting hardware.

Electronics & Instrumentation

Integrated and soldered the controller, motors, sensor connections, power system, and supporting electronics into a functional mobile platform.

Embedded Control

Programmed autonomous and BLE-controlled operating modes, translating sensor inputs and wireless commands into differential motor response.

03 · Mechanical Design & Fabrication

Packaging the Robot Around the System

The chassis and wheels were manufactured through additive manufacturing, allowing the geometry to be developed specifically around the electronics, motors, sensors, and battery package.

Packaging decisions included motor location, wheel alignment, sensor position, battery access, wiring routes, fastener locations, and maintaining enough rigidity for repeatable sensor-to-ground geometry.

Mechanical fasteners were used to keep major components secure while preserving access for troubleshooting and modification during development.

04 · Electronics Integration

From Components to a Working Robot

The electronics were assembled and soldered as part of the project rather than relying on a pre-built robotic platform.

The system combined the Arduino controller, motors, sensors, battery supply, and supporting electrical connections within a compact chassis.

Wiring layout became part of the mechanical design because connections needed to remain secure while still being accessible enough to debug throughout testing.

Progressive Integration

Individual functions were tested before combining the entire robot. Motors, sensors, wireless communication, and control behavior were each verified separately before full-system operation.

This made troubleshooting significantly easier because faults could be isolated to a specific subsystem rather than treating every unexpected behavior as a software problem.

05 · Autonomous Line Following

Using Sensor Feedback to Control Motion

In autonomous mode, onboard line sensors detected the robot's position relative to a black path.

The embedded program continuously interpreted the sensor response and adjusted the relative commands sent to the left and right motors.

Because the robot used differential drive, steering could be controlled by changing the relative wheel speeds rather than using a separate steering mechanism.

Reliable line following required repeated physical testing and tuning. Sensor placement, threshold behavior, motor response, vehicle speed, and steering corrections all affected whether the robot could recover smoothly from path deviations.

06 · Bluetooth Low Energy

Adding a Separate Wireless Control Mode

In addition to autonomous operation, the robot incorporated Bluetooth Low Energy communication for wireless manual control.

Commands transmitted over BLE were interpreted by the embedded program and converted into differential motor behavior, allowing forward motion, steering, and stopping without a physical connection to the robot.

Supporting both autonomous and wireless modes required the program to manage separate operating states and switch between different sources of control while maintaining predictable motor behavior.

07 · Instrumentation & Communication

Working With Sensors Beyond Basic Motion

Line Sensing

Used external sensing to detect the black path and provide the primary feedback signal for autonomous navigation.

Inertial Sensing

Worked with onboard inertial measurements to interpret motion and orientation information as part of the broader instrumentation system.

BLE Communication

Used Bluetooth Low Energy communication to exchange control and sensor information between devices rather than relying only on one-way remote commands.

08 · Testing & Debugging

Finding Problems Across Multiple Engineering Domains

The most difficult problems were often integration problems. Similar symptoms could originate from sensor readings, wiring, motor response, mechanical alignment, communication, or control logic.

Testing therefore progressed systematically from individual components to complete functions:

  • Motor direction and response
  • Sensor behavior
  • Electrical connections
  • BLE communication
  • Autonomous steering
  • Full-system operation

The robot was iteratively adjusted until it could repeatedly follow the intended path and respond reliably to wireless control inputs.

10 · Engineering Lessons

What Made the Project Valuable

Integration Matters

Mechanical, electrical, and software subsystems cannot be optimized independently when their behavior directly affects one another.

Debug Systematically

Breaking problems into individual sensors, motors, wiring, communication, and control functions made faults much easier to isolate.

Physical Testing Is Essential

Robot behavior depended on real motor response, sensor placement and chassis motion in ways that could not be fully predicted from code alone.
