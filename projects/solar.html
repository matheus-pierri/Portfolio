UBC Solar · Vehicle Dynamics

Front & Rear Wheel Hub Design & Validation

End-to-end mechanical design of custom lightweight front and rear wheel hub assemblies for UBC Solar's next-generation race car, integrating wheel, brake, bearing, upright, retention, manufacturing, and assembly requirements.

Role Vehicle Dynamics Designer
Design Ownership Front + Rear Hub Variants
Primary Tools SolidWorks · ANSYS · Excel · SKF SimPro
Focus Mechanical Design · FEA · Bearings · GD&T

01 · Project Overview

Owning Two Critical Rotating Assemblies

I have owned the mechanical development of both the front and rear wheel hub variants for UBC Solar's Cascadia vehicle, taking the system from benchmarking and concept development through detailed CAD, bearing selection, analytical calculations, FEA, manufacturing drawings, and assembly planning.

The hub is the central mechanical interface between the wheel, brake rotor, bearings and suspension upright. A change in one of those interfaces can propagate through nearly every dimension and load path in the assembly.

That became particularly important midway through the project. I had already developed and validated a complete spindle-based architecture when a sequence of vehicle-level changes began with the wheel selection, affected caliper packaging, and then required a different brake rotor configuration.

Rather than repeatedly patching the existing geometry, I used the new requirements as an opportunity to rethink the system and developed a new live-hub architecture in which the aluminum hub itself passes through and rotates within the bearing pair.

The front and rear versions share the same core architecture but were designed independently around their different packaging and loading requirements, giving me responsibility for both variants rather than simply adapting a counterpart's design.

The redesign required me to revisit the problem from the system level rather than simply modifying dimensions until the old architecture fit.

03 · Final Architecture

Live Hub with Back-to-Back Tapered Roller Bearings

The final design uses a custom 6061-T6 aluminum live hub supported by two SKF 33206 tapered roller bearings arranged back-to-back in an O configuration.

The O arrangement increases the effective spread between bearing load centers, providing the moment stiffness needed to react wheel loads while allowing both bearings to contribute to radial and axial load support.

The bearing outer rings are located by a shoulder inside the upright, while the rotating hub interfaces directly with the bearing inner rings. The inner-ring seats therefore require controlled interference to prevent relative motion during operation.

The brake rotor mounts to an integrated hub shoulder using four bolts and locknuts, while the wheel is secured through the outboard flange using a separate multi-bolt interface.

A stepped washer, M12 × 1.75 castle-nut interface and cotter pin provide axial retention. V-ring seals protect the exposed sides of the open tapered roller bearings from road contamination.

The front variant is shorter to satisfy suspension packaging, while the rear geometry uses the available space differently. Maintaining a common bearing and retention architecture reduced unnecessary system variation between the two designs.

04 · Bearing System Engineering

Designing the Bearing System From First Principles

Load Reactions

I built free-body diagrams and solved the force and moment equilibrium equations to convert tire-contact-patch loads into individual bearing reactions before entering them into bearing-selection and simulation tools.

SKF 33206 Selection

Bearing concepts were compared for packaging, radial and axial load capacity, moment stiffness, friction, serviceability and life. A matched pair of 33206 tapered roller bearings provided the best overall architecture.

Preload Strategy

A preload target of approximately 2.2 kN was estimated and checked using SKF guidance and SimPro modeling. The castle-nut system provides the controllable axial clamping required to establish the bearing condition during assembly.

Precision Fits

I evaluated bearing tolerances, contact pressure, friction, aluminum-steel thermal expansion and operating temperature before selecting an m6 hub fit and M6 upright fit.

Thermal Behaviour

Fits were checked at both a nominal 20°C assembly condition and an approximately 40°C operating condition so differential thermal expansion would not unintentionally eliminate or excessively increase the intended fit.

Lubrication & Sealing

Grease options were compared using predicted friction and bearing-life effects. SKF LGWM2 was selected together with V-ring seals to minimize losses while protecting the open bearing system.

Analytical Reaction Forces

For the severe combined loading case, I used equilibrium equations to solve the bearing reactions before relying on simulation. The spreadsheet model applies the braking, bump and cornering loads at the tire contact patch and accounts for the offsets between the tire and each bearing.

In one representative front-wheel case, this produced approximately 9.39 kN radial load at the inboard bearing, 7.22 kN at the outboard bearing, and 1.46 kN of axial load at the outboard bearing. Those reaction forces then became inputs to the SKF analysis and later structural models.

Why the Fits Required More Than a Lookup Table

Because the hub rotates inside the bearing inner rings, relative motion at that interface would risk fretting and loss of fit. The inner-ring interface therefore required interference.

I calculated the resulting interference pressure and frictional holding force, then repeated the geometry at operating temperature because the 6061-T6 hub expands faster than the steel bearing. The upright-to-outer-ring interface was treated differently: the goal was only a very light interference or near-nominal condition at operating temperature.

ANSYS Structural Analysis

I developed separate front and rear finite-element models using the combined 2g bump, 1g braking and 1g cornering design case. These loads were chosen to represent a deliberately severe simultaneous event rather than an ordinary operating condition.

FEA became an iterative design tool rather than a final pass/fail check. Low-stress regions were progressively reduced to remove unnecessary material, while fillets, flange transitions, bearing seats and highly loaded interfaces were adjusted when local stresses became limiting.

The final front design reached a minimum static factor of safety of 1.53, just above the 1.50 design target, while the rear variant retained a larger margin at 2.30. That difference reflects the different packaging and geometry of the two hubs rather than simply scaling one design.

Mass as a Design Constraint

Weight was tracked alongside structural performance throughout the design process because hub mass contributes directly to unsprung and rotating mass.

The final front hub component is approximately 0.99 kg and the complete front hub assembly is approximately 2.15 kg, representing a 14% reduction from the approximately 2.50 kg assembly used on the previous vehicle.

06 · Bearing Misalignment Validation

Checking Whether Structural Deflection Could Damage the Bearings

Tapered roller bearings can tolerate only limited angular misalignment. A hub that is structurally strong enough to avoid yielding could therefore still be unacceptable if elastic bending causes the two bearing seats to rotate too far relative to one another.

To model this without building a fully detailed bearing and upright contact model, I represented their radial compliance as elastic supports in ANSYS.

Upright support stiffness was estimated from the radial stiffness relationship k = EA / t. Because ANSYS required distributed stiffness in N/mm³, the area term was removed and the support input was derived from E / t, with an additional reduction factor used to avoid treating the full upright as a perfectly bonded rigid support.

Worst-Case Seat Deflection 4.28 μm

Differential displacement measured over half of the 25 mm bearing width:

θ ≈ 4.28 μm / 12.5 mm
θ ≈ 3.42 × 10-4 rad
θ ≈ 0.020° ≈ 1.18 arcmin

Result: approximately 1.18 arcmin of predicted misalignment versus SKF's approximately 4 arcmin allowable value.

The calculation was intentionally conservative because the outer-ring/upright face was treated as having effectively zero angular movement; including its deformation would reduce the relative bearing-ring misalignment further.

07 · Machine Design

Validating the Interfaces Around the Hub

Rotor Fasteners

Used Shigley's bolted-joint methods to calculate primary and secondary shear, preload, member stiffness, no-slip capacity, von Mises stress and fatigue performance for the four-bolt brake-rotor interface.

Frictional Load Transfer

Rather than designing the bolts to carry all braking shear directly through their shanks, preload was selected so much of the interface load could be transmitted by friction between the clamped surfaces.

Wheel Interface

The wheel attachment was evaluated against combined bump, braking and cornering forces, with bolt quantity and preload considered alongside flange geometry and assembly access.

Castle-Nut Threads

The M12 × 1.75 retention interface was checked for axial stress, external-thread pull-out and nut-thread pull-out under preload plus worst-case external axial loading.

Interference Fits

Fit calculations estimated contact pressure, frictional holding force and resistance to the fit being overcome, providing an analytical check alongside ISO fit selection.

Assembly Temperature

Differential thermal expansion was evaluated to determine whether bearing rings could be installed using controlled heating and cooling without requiring damaging installation forces.

08 · Manufacturing Readiness

Taking the Design Beyond CAD

Engineering Drawing

I produced the front hub manufacturing drawing to ASME Y14.5 conventions, defining the 30 mm bearing seat and m6 fit, geometric controls, M12 × 1.75 retention thread, four-hole rotor pattern, nine-hole wheel pattern, and other critical machining geometry.

The bearing seats received particular attention because dimensional and geometric error at those surfaces directly affects interference, rotating alignment, preload and bearing life.

Assembly Buildbook

I also developed the assembly buildbook covering parts and tools, safety, bearing preparation, lubrication, thermal installation, rotor mounting, sealing, castle-nut retention, cotter-pin locking and final wheel installation.

Writing the assembly procedure exposed practical issues that are easy to miss in CAD—tool access, installation order, temporary support, protecting bearing surfaces, checking fit during assembly, and ensuring that components can later be serviced.

Design for Manufacturing

The final geometry was designed around machining from 6061-T6 aluminum stock. Feature accessibility, tool approach, fillet size, stock diameter, tolerance cost and the number of machining setups were considered alongside structural optimization.

10 · Engineering Process

What Made the Project Valuable

Designs Must Survive Requirement Changes

The wheel-caliper-rotor cascade showed that a mature design can still need to be replaced when higher-level vehicle interfaces change.

Calculate Before Simulating

Free-body diagrams, bearing reactions, bolted-joint equations, fit calculations and thermal checks gave me independent ways to validate simulation outputs.

Simulation Should Guide Design

ANSYS was used iteratively to locate unnecessary material and limiting geometry rather than simply generating a final colourful stress plot.

Components Behave as a System

Bearing preload changes retention loads; fits change assembly; rotor geometry changes hub packaging; deflection affects bearing alignment. Individual calculations had to remain consistent with the complete assembly.

Supplier Data Matters

Bearing limits, tolerances, preload guidance, lubrication, sealing and life calculations relied on interpreting manufacturer information and incorporating it into the design.

Design Ends at Assembly, Not CAD

Drawings, tools, thermal-fit procedures, lubrication, fastener access and serviceability were treated as engineering requirements rather than tasks left until after design.
