Design & Manufacturing · Year-Long Team Project

RC Car Design & Manufacturing Upgrade

Year-long, capstone-style product-development project focused on redesigning an off-the-shelf RC vehicle through mechanical design, manufacturing-process development, prototyping, simulation, testing, cost analysis, and production planning.

Team 6 Students
Timeline Sep. 2025 – Apr. 2026
Production Scenario 20,000 Vehicles
Processes Casting · Thermoforming · Additive · Composites

01 · Project Overview

Redesigning a Product, Not Just a Part

This year-long course was structured to replicate the workflow of a larger engineering design project rather than a short laboratory exercise. Our six-person team began with a stock RC vehicle and progressively redesigned major systems around both product performance and realistic manufacturing constraints.

The final vehicle combined four major custom subsystems: sand-cast aluminum wheels, a self-righting roll cage, a thermoformed ABS aeroshell, and an adjustable structural chassis extension.

Each subsystem moved through concept generation, weighted selection, material and process selection, CAD, engineering analysis, physical prototyping, manufacturing trials, testing, redesign, cost estimation, and production planning.

The project concluded by integrating the subsystems into one manufacturable vehicle and developing a production strategy for a hypothetical 20,000-unit run. That requirement changed the way designs were evaluated: a successful prototype also had to make sense in terms of tooling, cycle time, labor, assembly, cost, repeatability and process risk.

02 · System Redesign

Four Subsystems, Four Manufacturing Problems

Cast Aluminum Wheels

Redesigned the stock wheel geometry around A356 aluminum sand casting, including draft, wall thickness, patterns, gating, risers, structural validation and tire retention.

Self-Righting Roll Cage

Developed a protective roll structure that uses vehicle geometry and center-of-mass behavior to return the car upright from the tested inverted condition.

Thermoformed Aeroshell

Designed an ABS vehicle shell and forming tool while studying draft, fillets, material thinning, webbing, venting, trimming and mold release.

Adjustable Chassis Extension

Developed a lightweight structural extension with multiple mounting positions and validated the geometry, material, bolted interfaces and production process.

03 · Aluminum Wheel Development

Learning Design for Casting Through Physical Failure

The wheel redesign had to balance structural performance, low mass, tire retention, castability and repeatable manufacturing. Multiple concepts were evaluated before selecting a five-circle geometry that provided smooth load paths while remaining practical to mold.

Material & Structural Analysis

A356 aluminum was selected through a weighted material-selection process. A severe impact-and-rotation FEA case predicted a maximum stress of approximately 2.25 MPa against an assumed yield strength near 165 MPa.

Manufacturing Iteration

The first casting attempts showed why manufacturability cannot be evaluated from CAD alone. Feature depth, sand stability, runner placement and mold formation all affected whether the geometry could actually be reproduced.

The casting process was changed between iterations. The first attempt failed, the second produced two usable wheels out of four, and the final iteration produced three successful parts from three attempts.

That sequence made the wheel one of the clearest examples in the project of physical manufacturing results directly driving design and process changes.

04 · Self-Righting Roll Cage

Turning a Failed Test Into a Functional Design

The roll cage was developed to protect the vehicle while also allowing it to recover from inversion without an external actuator.

The first halo-style prototype successfully protected the car but failed the self-righting requirement, leaving the vehicle balanced on two wheels during the worst-case inverted test.

The geometry was widened to increase the effective lever arm and alter the path of the vehicle's center of mass during rotation. The revised design consistently returned the car upright from the tested fully inverted condition.

Structural Validation

A conservative approximately 400 N impact case was evaluated using FEA. Peak stress was approximately 43 MPa compared with an assumed ABS yield strength of approximately 48 MPa, producing a relatively lean design rather than excessive structural margin.

Designing for Production

The final concept was redesigned as a modular injection-molded ABS assembly consisting of two halo components and a base. Tooling, ejector pins, alignment, cycle time, assembly and manufacturing cost were considered for the 20,000-unit production scenario.

05 · Thermoformed Aeroshell

Designing the Geometry Around the Process

The aeroshell was designed around vacuum thermoforming of 0.030-inch ABS sheet. This meant the appearance of the vehicle could not be separated from the way the plastic needed to stretch, contact the mold and release afterward.

Physical Process Development

Small forming experiments were used to examine draft angle, corner radii, mold height, female features, venting, material webbing and part release before finalizing the full-size mold.

Later iterations also addressed practical finishing issues such as trimming and cutting the formed shell, showing how apparently small process steps can become production bottlenecks.

Simulation-Guided Geometry

ANSYS PolyFlow was used to simulate the forming process and evaluate material movement and local thinning. The mold geometry was refined with smoother transitions and larger radii in areas where the process appeared more difficult.

Production Planning

The scaled production concept targeted approximately six minutes per shell with an estimated cost of about $5.6 per part. Trimming was identified as a significant contributor to the overall process time.

06 · Adjustable Chassis Extension

From Material Concepts to a Functional Installed Part

The chassis extension was intended to modify the overall vehicle length while maintaining alignment with the stock platform and allowing multiple mounting positions.

Early concepts considered composite manufacturing, including glass-fiber laminates. Material and process development included coupon manufacturing and testing before the final production concept evolved toward additive manufacturing.

The final design used PA6-GF25 chopped glass-fiber-reinforced nylon with a C-channel-inspired geometry.

Structural Validation

FEA predicted a maximum stress of approximately 21 MPa and a minimum factor of safety greater than 7.

Testing the Joint, Not Just the Part

Physical testing showed that the fastener configuration was just as important as the printed geometry. Two bolts allowed excessive movement, three significantly improved the joint, and four produced a rigid connection during vehicle testing.

Production Scale-Up

The final concept required approximately 35.8 minutes of printing per part. A conceptual 40-printer farm operating at 90% utilization was estimated to produce approximately 67 parts per hour at a calculated part cost of roughly $4.42.

08 · Manufacturing Experience

Using Multiple Processes Within One Product

Sand Casting

Pattern development, gating and riser design, sand molding, aluminum pouring, defect investigation and iterative process improvement.

Thermoforming

Mold design, draft, radii, venting, vacuum forming, trimming, webbing analysis and numerical process simulation.

Injection Molding Design

Redesigned the roll cage around scalable ABS injection molding, including modularity, tooling assumptions, ejection, alignment, cycle time and assembly.

Additive Manufacturing

Used 3D printing for prototypes, tooling, patterns and the final manufacturing concept for the chassis extension.

Composite Processing

Manufactured and tensile-tested glass-fiber composite coupons while evaluating a composite route for the chassis extension.

DFMA & Costing

Considered equipment, tooling, labor, cycle time, utilization, assembly, manufacturing risk and per-part cost throughout the development process.

09 · Final Integration

Bringing Four Manufacturing Systems Together

The final stage moved beyond validating individual components and focused on the vehicle as one manufacturing system.

The exploded CAD assembly was used to verify fitment, fastener locations, clearances and assembly order before completing the physical build.

Production planning then considered how wheel casting and machining, roll-cage molding, aeroshell thermoforming and chassis-extension printing could occur in parallel before converging at vehicle assembly.

This systems-level stage was especially valuable because the definition of a good design changed from simply "does the prototype work?" to "can this be produced repeatedly, assembled efficiently, validated and scaled?"

10 · Engineering Lessons

What Made the Project Valuable

Prototype Early

Casting defects, self-righting behavior, thermoforming release and joint stiffness all exposed issues that were difficult to predict from CAD alone.

Failure Is Engineering Data

Failed castings and the unsuccessful first roll-cage geometry directly informed the changes that produced successful later iterations.

Design for the Process

Geometry that works mechanically may still be unsuitable for molding, casting, thermoforming or additive manufacturing.

Validate the Interfaces

Testing the chassis fasteners showed that system behavior can be limited by the joint even when the component itself has a high structural safety factor.

Scale Changes the Answer

A prototype manufacturing method is not automatically the best choice for 20,000 units. Tooling, cycle time and equipment utilization can completely change the preferred process.

Manufacturing Is Part of Design

Material choice, tooling, process parameters, cost, manufacturability and assembly were treated as part of the engineering problem rather than downstream considerations.
