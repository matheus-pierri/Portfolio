Seaspan Shipyards · Forecasting & Rebaseline

SOC2 / SOC3 Forecasting Methodology

Developed a forecasting methodology from the ground up for supervisory and project-support work on the Joint Support Ship program. What began as a targeted JSS2 analysis expanded across four major account groups on two ships, supporting more than $10 million of additional baseline effort.

Initial Scope ≈ 70 Activities
Expanded Scope ≈ 250 Activities
Coverage 4 Account Groups · 2 Ships
Baseline Impact > $10M

01 · The Problem

When the Baseline No Longer Reflected the Remaining Work

The assignment began with a focused question: was the remaining baseline sufficient to support JSS2 supervisory and support activities through the rest of the project schedule?

Historical budgets had not always reflected the level or duration of support ultimately required. As the project progressed, a gap emerged between the remaining baseline, future schedule, current staffing behavior, and the work still requiring support.

There was no finished forecasting model waiting to be updated. I had to determine which project signals were actually meaningful, develop the methodology, challenge its assumptions, and create forecasts that were both analytically defensible and operationally realistic.

02 · Key Technical Insight

LOE Work Cannot Be Forecast Like Discrete Production Work

Discrete Work

Progress / Productivity Driven

Discrete activities earn value through measurable physical accomplishment such as fabrication, installation, testing, or commissioning.

Historical productivity and CPI can therefore provide useful evidence about how efficiently remaining physical work may be completed.

Level of Effort

Duration / Resource Driven

SOC2 and SOC3 largely represented supervisory, support, and service functions required while underlying project work remained active.

For this type of work, remaining schedule duration, expected staffing, recent burn, and future resource requirements were often more meaningful forecasting drivers than simply extending historical CPI.

One of my biggest practical EVM lessons was that the forecasting method should reflect how the underlying work is performed and earns value rather than applying one standard equation to every account.

03 · Developing the Methodology

Building the Forecast Around Multiple Signals

Historical Actuals

Used lifetime labor consumption to understand how each account had behaved relative to its original planning.

Recent Burn

Examined current labor consumption so older project conditions did not dominate assumptions about present staffing requirements.

Remaining Schedule

Used forecast completion dates and remaining duration to estimate how long supervisory and support resources would continue to be required.

Remaining Budget

Compared available baseline effort against the duration and staffing implied by the future project plan.

Resource Requirements

Evaluated the actual people and support functions expected to remain active rather than treating forecast hours as purely financial values.

Account Maturity

Considered how much confidence should be placed in historical behavior depending on how far an account had progressed.

The spreadsheet calculations were only one part of the work. The harder problem was understanding what the numbers represented: what each account supported, how long that requirement would remain, and whether the resulting forecast made operational sense.

04 · Converting Forecasts Into Workforce Demand

Connecting Remaining Work to Future Staffing

As the methodology developed, the analysis became as much a workforce-planning exercise as a cost forecast.

Remaining schedule duration and expected labor consumption were converted into time-phased resource requirements, allowing the forecast to show not only how much future effort was required, but when that effort would be needed.

This provided a more practical way to challenge forecast outputs: if the implied future staffing profile did not make sense relative to project activity, the underlying assumptions needed to be revisited.

05 · Iterative Development

Challenging the Method Instead of Defending the First Model

The methodology went through repeated review with my supervisor, Project Controls stakeholders, and PMO. Those discussions often focused less on whether a spreadsheet formula was mathematically correct and more on whether the forecast represented the actual project.

Does the schedule support it?

Checked whether the forecast remained consistent with expected activity duration and completion dates.

Does the staffing make sense?

Reviewed whether implied future resource levels were realistic for the work being supported.

Is historical CPI relevant?

Challenged whether past efficiency should actually drive future requirements for each type of work.

What changed recently?

Used recent burn and project conditions to prevent stale historical behavior from dominating the model.

Why is this account different?

Investigated outliers at the account level instead of forcing every area into the same forecasting rule.

Can the result be defended?

Refined assumptions until the final recommendation could be explained clearly to stakeholders reviewing the rebaseline.

06 · Activity-Level Timephasing

Turning the Forecast Into a Detailed Rebaseline Plan

Once the overall forecast was established, the work had to be translated back into detailed activity-level timephasing.

This was not a bulk spreadsheet adjustment. Every activity row in the timephasing workbook was reviewed individually and modified where required based on remaining schedule, account behavior, resource needs, and the intended future plan.

The same review process was repeated across the four major SOC2/SOC3 account groups covered by the expanded analysis.

The actual schedule updates were performed by Planning. My responsibility was the analytical forecasting work used to develop, test, and justify the revised requirements before those changes were incorporated into the project plan.

For me, the strongest indication that the methodology was useful was its expansion: an analysis originally built for one contained JSS2 problem was subsequently applied across equivalent account groups on both ships.

08 · Rebaseline Involvement

Supporting the Forecast Through the Broader Rebaseline Process

The forecasting methodology was only one part of the broader rebaseline effort.

My involvement extended into several connected activities, including forecast development, validation, activity-level timephasing, baseline-change coordination, data review, and supporting the movement from analytical recommendation into an approved future plan.

Working across those stages helped me understand the rebaseline as a connected process rather than a single spreadsheet exercise. Forecast assumptions ultimately had to remain consistent with schedule logic, resources, baseline governance, reporting, and implementation.

09 · Transferable Skills & Takeaways

What This Assignment Developed

Build Methods From Ambiguous Problems

There was no established model to follow. I had to translate an open-ended planning problem into a structured analytical method.

Understand Before Calculating

The difficult part was usually understanding how the account behaved and what future work required — not performing the arithmetic.

Use Models to Support Judgment

Forecasting outputs were treated as evidence to challenge assumptions, not as automatic replacements for operational judgment.

Iterate Without Attachment

The methodology improved because earlier approaches were changed when review and project evidence suggested better forecasting logic.

Communicate With Decision-Makers

Direct PMO involvement required explaining assumptions, methodology differences, outputs, and recommendations clearly enough to support planning decisions.

Good Analysis Can Scale

The progression from roughly 70 activities to approximately 250 across two ships demonstrated how a useful method can expand beyond the problem it was originally built to solve.
