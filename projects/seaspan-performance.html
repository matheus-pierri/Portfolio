Seaspan Shipyards · Performance Analysis

Variance Reporting & Performance Reconciliation

Prepared recurring JSS2 performance analysis while separating true operational execution from reporting, baseline, timing, and data-quality effects before communicating the resulting story to project leadership.

Reporting Cycle Weekly + Monthly
Primary Focus Normalized Performance
Analysis Activity + Discipline Level
Audience Director + CFO

01 · The Core Question

Were the Numbers Telling the Truth?

A major part of my recurring reporting work involved determining whether apparent project performance actually reflected productivity and execution.

CPI and earned-value trends could also move because of baseline changes, progress corrections, EV-rule changes, reporting timing, work-in-progress, rework, or other data-quality effects.

Instead of simply reporting the published metric, I learned to break it apart and determine which portions represented true operational performance and which did not.

02 · Normalizing Performance

Removing Non-Performance Effects From the Headline Metric

PMBCR Effects

Identified movements caused by approved baseline changes so they were not incorrectly interpreted as improvements or deterioration in execution.

Progress Corrections

Investigated overstated, understated, or incorrectly allocated progress and quantified the resulting earned-value distortion.

Reporting Timing

Considered cases where labor had been consumed before progress was recognized, creating temporary performance distortion.

EV-Rule / System Effects

Reviewed changes caused by system or measurement behavior rather than treating every movement in reported CPI as a productivity change.

Rework

Assessed the effect of repeated work on productivity and connected financial performance with execution quality.

Operational Performance

After separating those effects, focused the final narrative on productivity, field progress, labor effectiveness, and true execution trends.

03 · Progress Reconciliation

Quantifying the Distortion Instead of Just Flagging It

One recurring investigation compared progress reported by affected organizations against official company progress at the activity level.

Each week, I aggregated the differences by discipline and quantified:

  • Net overstated progress
  • Net understated progress
  • Resulting earned-value distortion
  • Impact on reported performance

The important question was not simply whether a reporting error existed, but how much it changed the story management was seeing.

Analytical Goal Reported CPI ≠ Always True CPI

Reconstruct what performance would have looked like if reporting and system distortions had not occurred.

04 · Weekly & Monthly Narratives

Turning Detailed Analysis Into a Short Performance Story

Weekly Reporting

Weekly reports focused on the most recent performance movement and the reasons behind it.

I wrote concise narratives summarizing the key numbers, direction of performance, major drivers, and any unusual one-time explanations that leadership needed to understand before interpreting the result.

Monthly Reporting

Monthly reporting went deeper by connecting the current period to broader trends such as historical performance, forecast movement, rework, WIP, productivity effects, and discipline comparisons.

Because much of the calculation work had already been developed through the weekly cycle, the monthly report focused more on interpretation and what the trends meant for overall project health.

05 · Transferable Skills & Takeaways

What This Work Developed

Root-Cause Analysis

I became much less willing to accept a metric at face value. When performance moved, the first question became why it moved and whether that explanation was supported by operational data.

Data Validation

Progress reconciliation taught me that mathematically correct reports can still be misleading when the source data, timing, allocation, or system behavior is wrong.

Analytical Judgment

Not every unusual value should be removed or normalized. I had to decide which effects represented legitimate execution and which represented reporting artifacts.

Technical Writing

I wrote much of the recurring commentary independently, translating detailed EVM analysis into short narratives that explained the numbers, causes, and implications without forcing the reader through the underlying calculations.

Executive Communication

The same analysis had to work at several levels of detail. A detailed variance report might contain activity-level investigation, while senior leadership needed only the few numbers and explanations that materially changed the story.

Management-Focused Analysis

The biggest lesson was that project controls should help management understand reality, not merely reproduce what the system reported. The value of the analysis came from making the metric more useful for decision-making.

06 · Executive Summary

Condensing the Report for Senior Leadership

Future Figure: Sanitized Raw vs Normalized Performance Trend

After each weekly variance report, I prepared an additional executive-level summary used by my supervisor, the Director of Program Controls, and the CFO.

The summary tracked both raw and normalized project performance, allowing leadership to see the headline result alongside a clearer estimate of underlying execution.

It also required compressing a large amount of investigation into a very small amount of text: key numbers, the most important drivers, and occasional justification for unusual movements.

This became one of my strongest exercises in communicating the same technical analysis differently depending on the audience.
