Performance Analysis & Reconciliation

Analyzed earned-value results to separate genuine operational performance from baseline, timing, progress, and reporting effects, then converted the findings into clearer performance metrics and concise management reporting.

Primary Metric CPI / Earned Value
Reporting Weekly + Monthly
Analysis Raw vs Normalized
Audience Program Leadership

01 · The Problem

A Metric Can Move Without the Work Actually Changing

CPI and earned-value trends are useful indicators, but the reported movement does not always come entirely from changes in operational execution.

Baseline changes, corrected progress, timing effects, rework, accounting movement, and other system updates can all influence the reported result.

My role was to investigate those movements and determine what portion represented true project performance versus what portion came from non-performance effects.

That distinction mattered because management decisions should be based on the underlying execution trend, not simply the first number displayed in the reporting system.

02 · Analytical Approach

Reconciling the Reported Result Back to Its Drivers

Baseline Changes

Identified PMBCR activity that changed planned value, earned-value structure, or budget without representing a corresponding change in physical execution.

Progress Corrections

Reviewed corrections to previously reported progress so historical reporting adjustments were not automatically interpreted as current-period performance.

Timing Effects

Investigated cases where cost or earned value shifted between periods and temporarily distorted the apparent performance trend.

System Effects

Considered how changes within the earned-value system itself affected the reported metric independently of shop-floor execution.

Rework & Execution

Distinguished legitimate operational drivers such as productivity loss or rework from reporting-related effects.

Account-Level Review

Traced major changes into the underlying accounts rather than relying only on the aggregate program-level CPI movement.

03 · Normalized Performance

Separating Reported CPI From the Operational Trend

Once the major non-performance effects were identified, I could construct a normalized view of the metric.

The goal was not to replace the official CPI or remove unfavorable results. The official metric remained the formal project result. The normalized view was an analytical tool for understanding what had actually changed operationally.

Comparing the two views helped answer a much more useful question: Did performance actually improve or deteriorate, or did the reporting environment change around it?

This made trend interpretation more defensible and helped prevent management attention from being directed toward the wrong cause.

04 · Root-Cause Analysis

Moving From “What Changed?” to “Why Did It Change?”

Identify the Movement

Start with the reported change in CPI, earned value, actual cost, or forecast and determine which areas contributed most.

Trace the Driver

Investigate the underlying accounts, baseline activity, progress movement, timing, and operational events responsible for the change.

Classify the Effect

Determine whether the movement represents execution, non-performance reporting effects, or a combination of both.

05 · Management Reporting

Turning Detailed Analysis Into a Few Useful Sentences

The analytical work ultimately fed into recurring weekly and monthly performance reporting.

Detailed account-level investigation had to be reduced into a concise explanation of the key number, what drove the movement, whether the effect was operational, and what management should pay attention to next.

I wrote and supported performance narratives for audiences ranging from account-level stakeholders to program leadership, with executive summaries contributing to reporting reviewed at Director and CFO level.

This required a different skill from building the analysis: deciding which details were essential and which would distract from the decision being communicated.

06 · Reporting Logic

What a Useful Performance Narrative Needs to Explain

What Happened?

State the material movement in CPI, cost, earned value, forecast, or another key performance indicator.

Why?

Identify the operational or non-performance drivers behind the movement rather than repeating the metric itself.

Is It Persistent?

Distinguish a one-period timing or reporting effect from a continuing productivity or execution trend.

What Matters Next?

Highlight the issue, account, forecast assumption, or trend that requires continued management attention.

07 · Transferable Skills & Takeaways

What This Work Developed

Question the First Answer

A KPI is the start of an investigation, not automatically the conclusion. Understanding the mechanism behind the number is often more important than the number itself.

Separate Signal From Noise

Normalization helped distinguish real changes in execution from system, timing, and reporting effects that could obscure the underlying trend.

Match Detail to the Audience

The same analysis may require account-level detail for one stakeholder and a two-sentence executive summary for another.
